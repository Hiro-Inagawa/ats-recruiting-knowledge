# LinkedIn discovery and professional evidence

This manual owns Chapters 11–16. Distinguish search discovery from inspection and evaluation; member profiles, saved résumé data and employer applications are separate records. Read [ATS mechanisms](ats-and-recruiting.md) for underlying process distinctions and the [source guide](../evidence/source-guide.md) for local attribution.

## Chapter 11: LinkedIn's role in sourcing and hiring

LinkedIn can help a recruiter discover people, inspect professional evidence, contact a possible candidate and revisit a previous relationship. Those activities sit alongside an employer's application and interview process. Being found is different from being considered for a funded vacancy. A profile view establishes a viewing event, not the viewer's motive. An InMail can begin a conversation without establishing that the sender owns a hiring decision.

Identify the surface before explaining what happened. Ordinary LinkedIn member search, Recruiter Lite and Recruiter have different capabilities. The current product comparison describes narrower network access for Lite and broader search, collaboration and integration capabilities for Recruiter. RPS and RPS+ are additional staffing-oriented variants. AI Search has its own product and language conditions. A person with “recruiter” in their title does not necessarily use any particular subscription. [Evidence: LI04, LI09, LI12.]

The practical sequence is discoverability, inspection, a relevant conversation, then the employer's agreed selection process. This is a reasoning model, not a mandatory funnel. A referral can enter during conversation, while an application can precede discovery. Previous applicants can be rediscovered in some configurations. The person still needs to understand which role, employer and next step the conversation concerns. [Evidence: LI10; Chapters 2, 8 and 9.]

### Application and decision guidance

Start with the decision you can influence. For a profile question, consider whether the professional identity is understandable, relevant experience has evidence, and the intended audience can reach that evidence. For an inbound message, ask which opportunity and what information the sender needs. For silence after applying, return to the ATS and status reasoning in Chapters 5, 8 and 10 rather than attributing it to LinkedIn ranking.

Open to Work preferences describe desired opportunities and a chosen audience. The current help page distinguishes public sharing, sharing with Recruiter users and preferences visible only to the member. It explicitly does not guarantee complete privacy for recruiter-only sharing. Treat selecting an audience as the member's decision. Knowledge assessment does not authorize changing the setting. [Evidence: LI13.]

If available time is limited, address the actual uncertainty. A broken portfolio route is an inspection problem. An obsolete availability preference is a preference problem. Neither requires a daily publishing schedule. Posting may have other purposes, but this collection does not establish a cadence that produces interviews. Do not substitute generalized engagement advice for the current hiring question.

### Worked example

Hypothetical situation: a senior designer receives several agency messages about junior on-site work. There is contact activity, but little role relevance. Check the stated target, current preferences and the actual messages before increasing profile keywords. If the user then says the messages followed a specialist community introduction, revise the explanation to that relationship route. Do not assume they originated in a Recruiter search. A relevant role description and agreed next step would be more useful evidence than the message count alone.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential practice is to distinguish discovery from evaluation and establish the actual opportunity. Advanced practice compares relevance across known inbound routes, using dated observations and a consistent definition of a suitable conversation. Common mistakes are treating every view as buying or hiring intent, assuming every recruiter has enterprise features, and accepting a privacy guarantee the platform does not make.

Unresolved questions include the sender's product, query and intent, the employer's funded need, and which process owns the next decision. Use workflow G for profile evidence and workflow E for an actual stalled application. [Evidence routes: LI04, LI09, LI10, LI12, LI13 in the local LinkedIn evidence guide.]

## Chapter 12: Recruiter search, filters and discovery

Search retrieves a candidate set under a query and accessible data. Filters can narrow that set. Ordering can depend on the searcher and context. These are separate mechanisms. A profile can be relevant to a role yet absent from one filtered result set. A high position in one person's search cannot establish its position for everyone.

Recruiter documentation describes Can have, Must have and Doesn't have controls, with temporal preferences such as current and past on applicable filters. Boolean field controls and a typed Boolean string are different interfaces. The help page describes quoted and Boolean strings in Job titles, Companies and Keywords, and separately lists filters with Boolean dropdown behavior. Use the documented field and interface rather than assuming every field accepts the same typed expression. [Evidence: LI08, LI17.]

Skills discovery is broader than an exact word in the Skills section. LinkedIn describes explicit skills, text mentions, shared résumé-derived skills and inferred skills. Its Skills Match applicant feature also considers location. Do not turn that feature into a universal qualification score or conclude that omitting one explicit skill excludes a person from every skill search. Natural-language AI Search is another documented discovery route with subscription conditions. Neither route proves that a particular recruiter used it. [Evidence: LI01, LI09.]

### Application and decision guidance

When assessing findability, use truthful role terminology and evidence. Ask whether an alleged problem concerns ordinary member search, Recruiter, an applicant list or an ATS search. If the actual query is unavailable, explain plausible mechanisms and identify the missing fact. Avoid diagnosing a private exclusion from a headline screenshot alone.

A keyword expression such as `("product designer" OR "UX designer") AND "design systems"` illustrates retrieval logic in an appropriate keyword context. It is not a command to paste into every filter and is not a recommended formula for all recruiters. OR broadens alternatives, AND asks for their combination, and NOT excludes terms. Excluding “junior” may remove a senior person who describes mentoring junior colleagues. Query matching remains distinct from level assessment. [Evidence: LI17; original example.]

Workplace type has a specific documented condition: the Recruiter filter returns candidates open to work with selected workplace preferences. That does not mean all remote recruitment uses that filter. Profile location, desired location, remote preference and permission to work in a jurisdiction are different facts. Changing a displayed location cannot create eligibility. [Evidence: LI14.]

### Worked example

Hypothetical observation: a designer's friend cannot find the profile under one query. The friend used ordinary member search, while an agency contacted the designer yesterday. The failed search does not prove global invisibility. Establish the exact query and surface, then inspect relevant evidence and preferences. If the user corrects the account type to Recruiter Lite, reassess the available network and controls instead of repeating the ordinary-search explanation.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential practice is accurate titles, supported skills and correct preference information. Advanced practice compares known search conditions or an authorized recruiter's feedback, keeping query scope and account context in the record. Common mistakes include keyword stuffing, universal ranking claims, replacing truthful experience with target titles, and assuming a search match equals a qualified candidate. LinkedIn warns against repeated keywords and inflated experience. [Evidence: LI11, LI15.]

Unresolved facts include the actual query, searchable fields, inferred skills and employer configuration. LI12's comparison table says Lite has no Spotlights, while LI04 and LI10 describe two Lite Spotlights. Keep that discrepancy visible and verify the actual account before prescribing an interface. No fixed filter count or entitlement is needed for the applicant reasoning here. Use workflows C and H. [Evidence routes: LI01, LI04, LI08, LI09, LI10, LI11, LI12, LI14, LI15, LI17.]

## Chapter 13: Profile sections and professional evidence

A useful profile lets a reader understand the work and locate evidence for it. The headline gives an initial professional description. About can explain the work's context and direction. Experience grounds claims in roles and contributions. Skills supply explicit labels, while Featured provides selected work samples. These editorial responsibilities are this manual's synthesis, not a platform ranking formula.

Build the evidence before choosing labels. For each important claim, identify an actual role or project, what the person did, what can be shown, and any limitation. “Design systems” might be supported by component decisions and governance. “AI product design” might be supported by a prototype and an evaluation exercise. Those examples do not automatically establish production deployment, organizational scale or outcomes.

LinkedIn's documentation describes skills associated with profile evidence and distinguishes explicit from inferred skills. A skill label helps describe experience but does not prove mastery. Inferred skills are platform interpretations rather than a field the member can precisely control. Use readable context instead of attempting to manufacture every possible inferred label. [Evidence: LI01, LI05.]

### Application and decision guidance

Give each relevant section a job. Use the headline to make the professional identity understandable. Use About to explain the kind of work and its relevance. Use Experience to state contribution and context. Support the most consequential claims with a reachable example. Preserve accepted titles and source facts. Where role naming needs clarification, explain scope truthfully instead of silently assigning a higher employer rank.

Select Featured items for the reader's evaluation task. A product design case can explain the problem, users, decisions and contribution. An AI example can identify what was tested and what remains a prototype. The platform allows selected media and links, but its help page says Featured content is not discoverable through search and requires a logged-in viewer. Treat Featured as evidence for inspection, not a guaranteed keyword channel or a complete public portfolio. [Evidence: LI02; Chapters 6 and 7.]

Public visibility is a separate decision. The public profile is simplified and not every section can be shown outside LinkedIn. Search-engine refresh can lag behind profile edits. Maintain an accessible portfolio route for the intended audience rather than assuming that every viewer sees the same profile. Exact privacy or interface changes require current verification and user authorization. [Evidence: LI07.]

### Worked example

Hypothetical profile: the headline claims “AI Product Designer,” Experience names only visual deliverables, and Featured contains an undocumented chatbot demo. Before adding more AI terms, inspect the actual project. If the designer conducted user research, designed escalation and tested failure cases, describe that contribution where appropriate and point to evidence. If the work is a personal concept, label it as such. A demo that produces an answer does not prove reliability or production adoption.

If the user declines a portfolio overhaul and has twenty minutes, assess one claim and its existing evidence route. A factual correction or clearer context may be enough. Do not insist on building a new case study unless the decision requires evidence that is actually missing.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential practice is accurate professional identity, understandable contribution and accessible proof. Advanced practice maps a small number of target-role requirements to different evidence depths across profile and portfolio, without duplicating a résumé wholesale. Common mistakes include unexplained labels, unsupported metrics, presenting team results as individual ownership, exposing restricted client material and using Featured as a search-ranking tactic.

Unknowns include the reader's view, feature access and the actual evidence supporting a claim. Specific field limits, endorsement weighting and Featured display inside each Recruiter variant are not established here. Use workflows G, H and I. [Evidence routes: LI01, LI02, LI05, LI07, LI11; ATS14/15 for employer-specific design evaluation.]

## Chapter 14: Recruiter inspection, credibility and measurement

Discovery creates an opportunity to inspect evidence. A reader may compare the stated role, experience, skills and work examples against a particular need. The manual recommends making that comparison easy, but does not establish a universal reading order or a fixed number of seconds recruiters spend on a profile. Published employer practices in Chapter 7 offer concrete examples of evaluation, not a common hiring rubric.

Credibility depends on whether the important claims withstand inspection. A polished description with no attributable contribution can leave the relevant question unanswered. A modest description with a clear case can supply useful evidence. Distinguish the quality of the explanation from the quality of the underlying work. If the work does not meet a stated requirement, improving wording cannot create the missing capability.

Platform indicators also need careful interpretation. Skills features provide additional context. Spotlights classify certain interests or interactions. LinkedIn itself describes verification as one trust signal and cautions against disqualifying applicants for lacking it. These signals do not replace a role-specific assessment. [Evidence: LI05, LI10.]

### Application and decision guidance

Investigate the specific point of uncertainty. Can the reader identify the candidate's contribution? Is the evidence accessible? Does it address the role's evaluation question? If a recruiter asks how a design system affected delivery, choose evidence about adoption, decision-making and constraints rather than listing only the component count. For an AI case, explain evaluation and failure boundaries rather than merely naming a model.

Keep measurement at the level the observation supports. Search Appearances supplies discovery statistics and available context about searchers. Profile views and other analytics describe different events. They are not one cohort moving through a measurable hiring funnel. LinkedIn says search order is personalized, so a few test accounts cannot establish global rank. [Evidence: LI06, LI15.]

Record the period, metric definition, relevant profile changes and suitable conversations actually received. Compare cautiously over time. A rise after an edit is an association, not proof that the edit caused it. More appearances with irrelevant messages can mean poorer targeting rather than improvement. A fall in views can coexist with one highly relevant conversation. Set a practical interpretation before declaring success.

### Worked example

Hypothetical weekly readings: 80 search appearances and 20 profile views, followed by 100 appearances and 25 views after a headline edit. The two metrics may represent different populations. Dividing them does not establish a discovery-to-view conversion rate. The change also overlaps with increased network activity. Record the observation and inspect whether the new conversations match the target roles. If the user says there were no messages, the next decision concerns evidence and relevance, not a guaranteed ranking repair.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential practice is to substantiate major claims and measure relevant events separately. Advanced practice uses stable observation periods, known definitions and qualitative recruiter feedback to test a bounded hypothesis. It does not manufacture causality from a small before/after sample. Common mistakes include declaring the profile broken because one metric falls, counting every message as a qualified opportunity and converting a historical job-search count into a current market forecast.

Unresolved questions include searcher intent, whether evidence was opened, the employer's criteria and analytics denominators. A profile may be credible and still have no suitable vacancy available. Workflows G and F help distinguish an evidence issue from an unsupported platform diagnosis. [Evidence routes: LI05, LI06, LI10, LI15; Chapters 1, 7 and 10.]

## Chapter 15: LinkedIn and ATS integrations, records and sharing

A LinkedIn profile, a résumé stored on LinkedIn, an employer's candidate record and a submitted application are separate objects. They can contain overlapping information without having identical contents or update behavior. Know which object is involved before explaining access, corrections or evaluation.

Recruiter System Connect supports particular integration capabilities. Unified Search documentation requires an RSC-enabled contract and an implemented integration. It describes searches involving both systems and customer enablement controls. This establishes a possible configured route, not a connection every ATS customer has. A supported ATS brand does not prove that the employer enabled it. [Evidence: LI03.]

LinkedIn's résumé-sharing help describes opt-in use of saved résumé data in recruiter discovery and states that this does not give recruiters the full document unless the member provides it, including through an application. It also disclaims complete privacy. This member setting is different from the customer-controlled résumé synchronization described in Unified Search. Do not collapse them into “all recruiters see all résumés.” [Evidence: LI16.]

### Application and decision guidance

For an access question, identify the account, source object, sharing setting and enabled integration. For a correction question, determine whether the error is in the profile, a saved résumé, an ATS field or an already-submitted application. Ask which one the employer actually received. Do not promise that changing the profile updates every downstream record.

For an employer claiming rediscovery, LinkedIn documents prior interactions and an additional ATS applicant route when RSC is enabled. That does not establish the full history visible to a particular reviewer or the exact data they inspected. Return to permissions and record scope in Chapters 2 and 10. [Evidence: LI10.]

An assessment can explain the choices around résumé sharing without changing them. Review what a résumé contains, whether it is current and whether the member wants the documented discoverability route. Consider retained older versions and truthful differences between tailored documents. Do not upload, delete or enable anything just because a source presents an instruction sequence.

### Worked example

Hypothetical claim: “This company uses Workday, so it can see my entire LinkedIn profile and all my uploaded résumés.” The system name does not establish an integration, permission or shared object. Establish the application route and known record before inferring access. If the user provides a screenshot identifying an enabled RSC connection, narrow the uncertainty to that feature's configured data and permissions. An integration label still does not prove that a reviewer opened a particular item.

Another hypothetical issue: the member fixes a date on LinkedIn after submitting a résumé with the old date. Preserve what was actually submitted and determine whether the employer permits an update. Do not assume the old document changed or instruct the person to conceal the difference.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential practice is naming the object and distinguishing sharing from submission. Advanced practice records lineage between profile, saved résumé, submitted file and employer correction, with dates and evidence. Common mistakes are universal access claims, assumed automatic propagation, equating a stub profile with an application and treating an integration's technical specification as evidence of employer use.

Unknowns include customer enablement, permissions, field mappings, retention and synchronization state. This edition provides mechanism reasoning, not a privacy-law assessment or instructions to configure an employer system. Exact operation requires current official guidance. Use workflow I and, for a record problem, workflow B. [Evidence routes: LI03, LI10, LI16; Chapters 2, 3 and 10.]

## Chapter 16: Consistency across profile, résumé and portfolio

Consistency means that the same professional history and claim can be reconciled across assets. It does not require identical wording or identical detail. A targeted résumé selects evidence for one opportunity. A profile can describe broader experience. A portfolio develops selected work in depth. They should agree on the facts relevant to verification and evaluation.

Use the facts the user has confirmed and the current inspected assets as the basis for comparison. Dated snapshots and drafts can disagree with each other. They preserve history, not permission to overwrite current titles or revive an old positioning decision. This knowledge provides a comparison method rather than adjudicating a person's current profile from historical notes.

A consistency review focuses on roles, dates, contribution, project state and evidence. Distinguish an official title from an explained functional scope when that distinction matters. A design concept can become a deployed product later, so differently dated documents need a chronology before declaring a contradiction. Team scale and outcome claims need their own support. Accurate experience remains a platform requirement. [Evidence: LI11; Chapters 1 and 7.]

### Application and decision guidance

Compare one consequential claim at a time. Record the wording and date in each asset, identify the source fact and classify the difference. It may be compatible emphasis, a stale version, an ambiguous clarification or a factual contradiction. Compatible emphasis can stay. A stale artifact needs a bounded update if authorized. A contradiction needs the person's source facts before rewriting.

Prioritize differences that could change the reader's assessment: employment dates, titles and level, authorship, deployment state, role scope and claimed outcomes. A different order of skills or shorter project description may have little consequence. Do not create busywork by enforcing word-for-word symmetry.

Respect timing and access. A sent résumé is a historical submission, while the live profile may change. The public view can differ from the signed-in view, and shared résumé data can have its own discovery route. Keep the record of what was provided rather than assuming every viewer sees the latest version. [Evidence: LI07, LI16; Chapter 15.]

### Worked example

Hypothetical assets: the profile says “led product design,” the résumé says “sole designer,” and the case explains collaboration with engineers and a product manager. These can be compatible if leadership means design direction rather than people management. Ask about the actual responsibility if it changes the role assessment. Do not automatically downgrade the claim or inflate it into management.

A second example concerns an AI project. A January résumé describes a prototype, while an October case says deployed. Verify the deployment date and responsibility. If the project shipped in August, the difference has a chronological explanation. If it remains a prototype, correct the unsupported deployment claim when authorized. If the user rejects a broad rewrite, limit the review to that material fact.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential practice is keeping consequential facts explainable and backing claims with current evidence. Advanced practice maintains dated claim records and submitted versions, making later recruiter questions easier to answer. Common mistakes are treating every wording difference as dishonesty, allowing title or outcome inflation for search terms, silently changing accepted personal facts and using archived profile drafts as current authority.

Unknown facts include the latest live profile, the actual submitted résumé and the person's approved source facts. Ask for the specific missing fact rather than assuming the archive is current. Use workflow I, then workflow H if authorized role-focused wording is needed. [Evidence routes: LI07, LI11, LI16; Chapters 1, 7, 10, 13 and 15.]
