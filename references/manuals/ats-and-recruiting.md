# ATS and recruiting mechanisms

This manual owns Chapters 1–10: hiring requirements, candidate records, parsing, discovery, review, AI assessment, interviews and process interpretation. Use the [reading routes](../reading-routes.md) to choose a sequence. Supporting source explanations are in the [source guide](../evidence/source-guide.md).

## How to use this knowledge

Start with the decision, then read the relevant chapter and workflow. Distinguish four kinds of information: documented product capability, a particular employer's published practice, original reasoning in this manual, and facts supplied by the user. Hypothetical examples illustrate reasoning; they are not measured hiring outcomes. The Ashby keynote material is publisher transcript narration, not independently observed screen behavior.

Do not turn a product feature into a claim about every employer. A system name alone cannot reveal enabled features, permissions, evaluation criteria or whether anyone read a particular application. No universal ATS score, keyword target, success probability or waiting period follows from these sources. Verify current product instructions before relying on an exact interface or operational requirement. Hiring law and personal eligibility require their own current authoritative evidence.

A local [recruiter walkthrough companion](../examples/recruiter-walkthroughs.md) adds a conventional Workable guided tour and seven visually sampled frames from a separate 2024 Ashby release video. The tour reached its closing screen; the video was not continuously watched or heard. This does not change the keynote transcript's evidence status. [Evidence: DEMO01, DEMO02.]

### Few suitable conversations: choose the next controllable step

Use this diagnostic when applications or LinkedIn activity are producing few relevant conversations. It does not infer an unseen employer decision or guarantee a response. Inputs are the actual target role, a small dated set of applications or messages, the versions supplied, existing evidence and available time. If these are missing, establish one concrete case before proposing a broad rewrite.

| Check | Evidence to inspect | Decision and stopping condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target fit | Stated required conditions against truthful experience; direct, transferable or missing evidence | If a mandatory condition is genuinely absent, choose a better-fit role or ask about ambiguity. Stop polishing wording as a substitute for that condition. Use Chapters 1/7 and workflow A or H. |
| Submitted record and route | Receipt, requested answers, actual file/version and any observed missing field | Correct an observed problem through an authorized route. A local text check cannot prove employer parsing. If there is no evidence of a record problem, stop attributing silence to parsing. Use Chapters 2/3 and workflow B. |
| Relevant claim and proof | One material contribution, its context and an accessible evidence route for the intended reader | Clarify real work or fix inaccessible proof when authorized. If the capability is missing, preserve the gap. Stop after the bounded claim is accurate, understandable and supported. Use Chapters 13/14 and workflow G. |
| Opportunity and communication | Known role state, relevant messages, agreed next step and any promised date | Distinguish unsuitable contact from a suitable opportunity. For a missed commitment, choose an answerable question when outreach is authorized. Otherwise retain the unknown rather than assigning a cause. Use Chapters 8/11 and workflow E. |

Choose one change because an observed issue supports it, not because every silent application requires a profile overhaul. Record the issue, action, observation period and relevant event separately. A before/after change is not causal proof; no standard response quota or test duration is established here. If all controllable checks are satisfactory, continue with suitable opportunities or obtain specific feedback rather than repeatedly rewriting the same material.

Hypothetical twenty-minute case: a role explicitly requires design-system adoption work, while the profile names only component creation. Existing permitted evidence shows contribution to adoption guidance and engineering collaboration. Review that one claim with workflow G and, if drafting is authorized, clarify the contribution and link to the existing case. Leave unrelated sections alone. If the user then says the adoption work was someone else's, revise the recommendation: do not add the claim; map the actual contribution or retain the gap. Fewer messages alone would not justify this edit. [Synthesis: Chapters 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 11, 13 and 14; workflows A, B, E, G and H.]

### Mechanism comparison

| Mechanism | Typical input | Output | Decision owner and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parsing | Uploaded document | Extracted profile fields, partial extraction or failure | Software extracts; extraction is not qualification. A permitted user may correct fields. |
| Search | Query and accessible indexed fields | Matching records, sometimes relevance ordering | Searcher chooses query and scope. A returned match is not a hiring recommendation. |
| Filter | Selected field values, tags, stages or criteria | A subset of records | User chooses filters; missing or inaccurate fields affect inclusion. |
| Eligibility rule | Answers to configured questions | Tag, advance or reject action | Employer configures conditions; a supported rule can execute automatically. |
| AI assessment | Defined criteria and available candidate evidence | Suggested evaluation, rationale, unknown/skipped states in some products | Product-specific human or automated actions must be checked separately. AI output is not proof of ability. |
| Human review | Documents, answers, prior feedback, job context | Notes and an advance/reject/hold decision | Permitted reviewer acts within employer process. A decision can still be mistaken or incomplete. |
| Interview evaluation | Interview evidence and rubric | Scorecard or recommendation | Interviewer assesses assigned criteria; hiring owner combines evidence and approvals. |

Evidence: ATS01, ATS02, ATS04, ATS09, ATS10, ATS11, ATS20, ATS22. The table is a synthesis, not a common architecture guaranteed across all systems.

## Chapter 1: Hiring process, roles and requirements

Hiring connects a business need to a role, candidate evidence, evaluation and an authorized decision. An applicant encounters only part of that process. A recruiter may coordinate screening and communication; a hiring manager may own the role decision; interviewers supply evidence; administrators control configuration. Actual responsibilities overlap and vary. Use these role descriptions to formulate questions, not to presume an employer's organization chart.

An important distinction is between a public requirement and how the team uses it. Some requirements are essential constraints, some describe preferred experience, and some are evaluated through work examples. GitLab's published design hiring guide connects a recruiting kickoff, must-have and preferred attributes, scorecards and budgeted-hire justification. That is an inspectable employer example, not a universal rulebook. [Evidence: ATS15.]

### Application and decision guidance

Before tailoring evidence, identify the actual work, level, location and stated eligibility conditions. Separate facts you can demonstrate from requirements that remain ambiguous. If a posting asks for experience with complex enterprise workflows, find a project showing the complexity, users, constraints and your contribution. If it asks for a mandatory authorization or license you do not have, rearranging résumé language cannot resolve that condition. Personal or legal answers remain the applicant's responsibility.

Use the current role document and employer instructions. A historical job-family page can explain a hiring model but cannot establish that a specific vacancy is still funded or unchanged. When information conflicts, record the conflict and seek the fact that affects the decision rather than choosing the most convenient interpretation.

### Worked example

Hypothetical role: senior designer for a regulated operations product. Applicant evidence: substantial workflow design, no direct regulated-industry project. The decision is whether the employer requires that domain or treats it as preferred. Preserve the real background, explain transferable workflow experience and mark the domain gap. If the recruiter later confirms that direct domain experience is mandatory, revise the fit assessment. Do not manufacture a sector history.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: record role identity, actual requirements, evidence and unknowns. Advanced: map which stakeholder evaluates which criterion and where the employer's published process differs by level. Common mistakes include treating every bullet as an automatic rejection condition, treating recruiter enthusiasm as final approval, and assuming a public posting proves an active hiring decision. Open question for a particular role: which requirements and approvals actually govern it? Use workflow A.

## Chapter 2: Candidate intake and records

A résumé file, candidate profile and application to a particular job are related objects, not interchangeable evidence. Documents can be attached while structured fields remain incomplete. A candidate can have information from multiple routes: an application, sourcing, referral, agency submission or previous consideration. The reviewer may see more than the current PDF, subject to permissions and available records.

Greenhouse documents application attachments, question responses and permitted access to other applications. Workable documents a profile with activity, files and candidate details. These are examples of possible record contents, not confirmation that a particular reviewer opened them. [Evidence: ATS01, ATS20.]

The observed Workable guided tour separates Profile, Answers, Timeline, Communication, Review, Comments, Offer and Files. Selecting a job and stage precedes opening the record. A tab or source label tells you where information is presented; it does not certify the evidence or prove that a reviewer inspected it. Conditional-tab guidance was read in the demo, without testing backend permissions. [Evidence: DEMO01.]

### Application and decision guidance

Make the required application complete on its own: usable contact details, accurate answers, the requested résumé and an accessible portfolio where allowed. A portfolio link is a route to evidence; it does not establish that external page content was imported, indexed or visited. Follow the actual submission instructions rather than assuming all systems handle attachments alike.

When there may be a duplicate record or stale file, identify the job, email used, submission time and known document version. Do not create extra applications simply to force visibility. A permitted employer user may have record-management options, but an applicant cannot assume those actions occurred.

### Worked example

A designer first enters through an agency, later applies directly, and receives a message referring to an older résumé. The supported observation is that the message uses older information. Possible explanations include an earlier attachment, a separate record or a reviewer using an old download. Establish the record and document version before diagnosing a merge error.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: keep the submitted file and role-specific receipt. Advanced: maintain an evidence timeline of versions, routes and corrected information. Common mistakes include confusing a successful upload with a complete profile, assuming a referral replaces required application steps, and assuming a candidate source label captures every interaction. Workable documents source attribution conventions, including first visits and later referrals, which can differ from an applicant's narrative. [Evidence: ATS23.] Unknown: the actual record history and permitted visibility. Use workflows B and E.

## Chapter 3: Parsing and structured information

Parsing attempts to extract information into fields. It can fail wholly or partially while leaving the document attached. Greenhouse's troubleshooting article describes both these outcomes and manual correction. Its documented formatting risks include image-based files, complex layouts and information in headers or text boxes. Its file-size limit is product-specific and must not be treated as an industry limit. [Evidence: ATS10.]

### Application and decision guidance

Use the employer's accepted format and verify that important text is selectable and has a sensible extraction order. Clear names, dates, employers, role titles and sections make the document easier to inspect. These checks reduce avoidable ambiguity; they do not certify the employer's parser. Keep design presentation in the portfolio when a visually complex résumé compromises readable content.

If an application form displays imported fields, inspect and correct them before submission. Where no preview is available, distinguish a local extraction check from a real product test. A plain-text extraction that looks correct is useful evidence about the file, not proof that every parser will behave the same way.

### Worked example

A PDF visibly says “Senior Product Designer,” but the imported role is blank. The file opens, the original text is selectable, and other imported fields are correct. This supports a partial import problem. Correct the displayed role if the form allows it; preserve the actual title. It does not support “the résumé was automatically rejected.” If all text is an image, a text-based export is a reasonable next preparation step, subject to actual file instructions.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: inspect the file and any imported fields. Advanced: compare visible text, extracted order and employer-side import feedback to isolate the failure. Avoid claiming that all columns always fail, every PDF succeeds, or parsing is a pass/fail employment judgment. Avoid keyword stuffing as a repair for corrupted text. Unknown: whether the employer parsed this version successfully and which fields it uses. Use workflow B.

## Chapter 4: Search, filters and rediscovery

Search answers a retrieval question: which accessible records match this query? It does not answer whether a person can do the work. Filters restrict a result set by selected values; sorting changes its order. Those are distinct operations even when the interface combines them.

Greenhouse supports Boolean search in a full-text mode. Workable describes Boolean search across candidate information while excluding comments and custom fields from its documented text-search scope. A visible field is therefore not automatically a searchable field. iCIMS' FAQ also distinguishes person, job and recruiting-workflow search and warns that behavior depends on configuration and permissions. [Evidence: ATS11, ATS22, ATS13.]

### Application and decision guidance

Use recognizable role and skill terminology truthfully, with supporting context. A term in a résumé can help a particular query find it, but that does not justify repeating a word to seek an imagined universal rank. Structured values such as location and experience can matter independently from prose. Keep those values accurate.

For a hypothetical query `(“product designer” OR “UX designer”) AND “design systems”`, OR provides alternatives and AND requires both groups under Boolean semantics. Whether this syntax works, which fields it covers and how relevance is ordered must be checked in the actual product. Greenhouse's article contains an internally ambiguous sentence in its AND explanation; this manual uses the consistent both-terms requirement and records that wording problem in the evidence guide.

### Worked example

A recruiter searches for “accessibility” and a designer's résumé only says “inclusive experiences.” If accessibility work actually occurred, adding its accurate name alongside the task and evidence can clarify the record. If it did not, adding the keyword creates a false claim. A missing result could also reflect filters, indexing scope or inaccessible records, so one query cannot diagnose the whole process.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: separate retrieval from qualification and use supported terminology. Advanced: compare alternative queries and examine which field changed inclusion. Common mistakes include assuming all profile content is indexed, treating a rank percentage as ability, and confusing removal by a location filter with AI rejection. Unknown: the actual query, scope and filters. Use workflow C.

## Chapter 5: Review queues, eligibility and human decisions

Application review can involve a person opening documents and answers, applying an evaluation and taking an action. Greenhouse documents advance/reject controls subject to permissions. Separate application rules can automatically tag, advance or reject based on configured answers to job-post questions. These are distinct mechanisms; neither requires a universal résumé score. [Evidence: ATS01, ATS02.]

Workable provides a second conventional workflow example: users select a job stage, inspect candidate information and take permitted actions. Its documentation also describes optional evaluation and agentic sorting modes. The presence of those modes does not establish which mode an employer enabled. [Evidence: ATS20.]

### Application and decision guidance

For application questions, answer the actual question accurately. A long portfolio cannot substitute for an unmet essential eligibility constraint. When the wording is ambiguous, determine whether the uncertainty is a factual, legal or role-definition issue. Do not guess personal eligibility or change an answer merely to bypass a rule.

For reviewer-facing evidence, reduce the work required to connect a requirement to a concrete example. This is a communication recommendation, not a guarantee of attention. Distinguish “not shown in these materials” from “cannot do it.” An applicant can strengthen the former with truthful evidence; the latter requires actual ability or a different opportunity.

### Worked example

An application is rejected within minutes. Known facts: submission and notification times. Possible mechanisms include a configured question rule, human or bulk action, or earlier information. Timing alone cannot select one. Review the exact submitted questions and any explicit explanation. A documented incompatible answer warrants a fit reassessment; no such evidence warrants an unknown cause, not a keyword diagnosis.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: distinguish question rules, review and communication. Advanced: map the triggering input, action and evidence needed to verify a proposed mechanism. Avoid assuming fast means AI, slow means human, or every listed requirement triggers rejection. Unknown: the rule configuration, action history and reviewer evidence. Use workflows D and F.

## Chapter 6: AI-assisted assessment

AI-assisted review can compare available evidence with employer-defined criteria and generate a suggested assessment or rationale. Ashby's 2024 release describes this approach, citations, unknown/skipped states and reviewer control. Those documented safeguards are product claims; they do not establish perfect accuracy or unbiased outcomes. [Evidence: ATS04.]

The 2026 Ashby keynote transcript supplies an example of a recruiter considering adjacent experience rather than treating an unmet literal criterion as decisive. This is narrated demonstration behavior, not a measured study of hiring outcomes. Scheduling and career-builder previews in that keynote are not proof of current release. [Evidence: ATS05.]

Selected frames from the separate 2024 release video show editable criteria, criterion badges, résumé-based explanations, a correction dialog and distinct advance/reject controls. One positive rationale includes an inferred conclusion that requires checking its time basis and attribution against the complete evidence. Neither its correctness nor product accuracy was independently validated. The visible Meets 3 of 4 count is a demo-specific criterion count, not a universal ATS score. [Evidence: DEMO02.]

### Application and decision guidance

Ask what was assessed, against which criteria, using which material, and who made the next decision. A generated explanation can point to text while still misinterpreting it. Missing evidence, contradictory evidence and a negative judgment are different states. Do not infer the employer's AI use from a product logo or a rejection email.

For your own AI-related experience, distinguish an experiment from deployment. State your contribution, evaluated behavior, limitations and user context. “Built an AI prototype” does not establish production reliability or ownership of model engineering. This distinction is original evidentiary reasoning in the manual, not a special ATS scoring rule.

### Worked example

A criterion asks for incident-management experience. The résumé describes complex support workflows without naming incident ownership. The available text supports related experience but may not establish direct ownership. Present the actual work and gap. If the user later supplies a project where they owned the relevant workflow, revise the assessment and cite that fact; do not defend the earlier missing-evidence conclusion.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: distinguish assessed text from actual ability and AI output from action. Advanced: inspect rationale, evidence scope and override route when those are actually available. Avoid treating a score as a universal standard, assuming citations ensure correctness, or turning a vendor efficiency story into an expected result. Unknown: employer criteria, enabled automation and actual decision owner. Use workflow F.

## Chapter 7: Interviews, scorecards and design evidence

An interview can test criteria that a file could not establish. Greenhouse documents scorecards built around predetermined attributes and interview feedback. GitLab's design guide asks evaluators to ground assessments in available evidence and leave uncovered attributes unanswered. These sources support a distinction between an observed weakness and a topic that was never assessed. [Evidence: ATS09, ATS15.]

For design, prepare evidence of reasoning and contribution rather than relying solely on attractive outputs. A case can explain the problem, your responsibility, constraints, choices, collaboration, what shipped or was tested, and the limits of observed results. Adapt its emphasis to the role rather than claiming one universal portfolio format. GitLab's job-family page provides an employer example of case discussion and different expectations for broader senior roles. [Evidence: ATS14.]

### Application and decision guidance

Choose evidence that answers the actual evaluation question. For hands-on work, show the decisions and artifacts you produced. For a senior or cross-team role, also explain how you resolved competing constraints and influenced decisions. Years of experience, a title and polished screens alone cannot establish those responsibilities. Do not invent impact metrics when only qualitative learning is available.

Explain team outcomes and individual contribution separately. Protect confidential material by using authorized descriptions or redacted evidence; this may leave some claims less directly inspectable. Say what the public case can establish rather than asserting unseen evidence. If a discussion asks about a tool, connect it to actual use, choices and limitations.

### Worked example

Hypothetical candidate shows an attractive dashboard. The target role emphasizes complex operational systems. Strengthen the case with a workflow map, an important failure mode, a design tradeoff, collaboration with engineering and an observed result. If the project was a concept, label it a concept. If it was shipped but outcomes were not measured, describe delivery and learning without inventing conversion improvements. For AI work, identify the model-assisted task, evaluation boundary and your responsibility.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: prepare accurate examples linked to requirements and distinguish your work from the team's. Advanced: anticipate alternative interpretations and show the evidence behind tradeoffs. Common mistakes include treating a scorecard number as objective truth, assuming every employer uses the same rubric, equating tool lists with competence and overclaiming causal impact. Unknown: the panel's actual questions and rubric. Use workflow A, then F for assessment claims.

## Chapter 8: Pipeline, scheduling and communication

The observed Workable tour distinguishes communication, internal comments, review records and a conditional offer area. These are separate records and actions, with guidance about visibility and approval. A record's status does not by itself establish what the candidate was told. Visibility statements in the tour were not independently tested and are not legal disclosure guarantees. [Evidence: DEMO01.]

A stage records process state in a system; it may not capture the latest human decision. Greenhouse documents that active candidates can remain active after a job closes and provides guidance for correcting inaccurate hiring data. Its rejection workflow can send, delay or omit an email. Candidate status, internal action and notification time therefore need separate interpretation. [Evidence: ATS17, ATS18, ATS19.]

### Application and decision guidance

Record exact observations: role identity, last confirmed event, message wording, dates and any promised next step. A portal saying “in review” supports only the displayed status. It does not prove that review has begun, that the role remains open or that the candidate remains competitive. Likewise, silence cannot distinguish scheduling load, missing feedback, changed priorities, a closed vacancy or an uncommunicated decision.

Choose the next step from useful evidence. If a promised date has passed, a concise factual inquiry may resolve uncertainty, but this knowledge does not send it. Continue making other application decisions without assigning invented probabilities to a stale stage. Do not impose a universal follow-up schedule; consider the agreed timeline and communication channel.

### Worked example

A candidate completed an interview, was promised an update Friday, and sees the same portal stage on Tuesday. The missed promise is known; its cause is unknown. The useful inquiry is whether the role and next step are still active, not an accusation about automated rejection. If the employer later says the role was paused, update the explanation and stop treating the stage as an active forecast.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: distinguish displayed state, confirmed action and communication. Advanced: build a timeline and note conflicting evidence without resolving it by assumption. Avoid counting every open application as a likely interview or assuming a closed job clears all candidates automatically. Unknown: current vacancy state and internal action history. Use workflow E.

## Chapter 9: Referrals, agencies and prior applicants

Recruiting relationships can introduce context or a different intake route. They do not erase employer evaluation or prove a decision. An agency submission and a completed employer application can be separate steps. Flowserve's publicly hosted 2022 Workday guide provides one concrete example: agency submission leads to employer review before the applicant completes the employer process. It describes that tenant's historical workflow, not every Workday installation. [Evidence: ATS24.]

Existing records can also be considered for a later role. The Ashby keynote narrates rediscovery, while Greenhouse documents prior application history subject to permissions. Neither establishes that a particular employer will retain or revisit your record. [Evidence: ATS05, ATS01.]

### Application and decision guidance

When a recruiter or referrer is involved, identify the employer, role and the action actually completed. “I passed your profile along” does not establish employer receipt, application completion or interview selection. Keep accurate dates, materials and any confirmed representation arrangement. Contractual agency questions belong to the relevant engagement authority, not inferred ATS behavior.

For repeat interest, prepare current evidence and explain what changed: experience, availability or role relevance. Do not assume an old negative decision applies forever or a referral guarantees advancement. Avoid sending duplicate submissions through multiple intermediaries without understanding the actual arrangement.

### Worked example

An agency says a designer was submitted, but the employer later asks them to apply. These statements need not conflict: the initial submission may be an introduction requiring a separate application. Confirm the exact role and employer instructions. This is a possible workflow supported by an employer example, not proof that the same workflow applies here.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: distinguish introduction, submission, application and employer decision. Advanced: maintain the evidence timeline across intermediaries and flag stale claims. Avoid reading an attribution label as a complete relationship history. Unknown: representation, current record, access and employer workflow. Use workflows A and E.

## Chapter 10: Configuration, permissions and limits of inference

The employer's configuration matters as much as the product name. Available features can depend on subscription, user permissions, job setup, stage and enabled integrations. Source pages describe capabilities under stated conditions. They rarely reveal a private employer's use. Workable documents field visibility and action differences; Greenhouse documents permission-dependent actions; iCIMS explicitly warns about configured differences. [Evidence: ATS21, ATS01, ATS13.]

### Application and decision guidance

Evaluate claims at the right level. “This product supports Boolean queries” is a capability claim. “This employer searched my profile using this query” is an event claim requiring additional evidence. “That query caused my rejection” adds a causal claim requiring still more. Keep these levels separate.

Use the smallest supported explanation. A paused job, changed requirements, internal candidate, limited reviewer capacity or inaccurate record may be relevant possibilities; none becomes a diagnosis without role-specific evidence. GitLab publishes an adapted internal-candidate process, and Greenhouse describes several reasons a job might close. These examples establish that such process differences exist, not how often they occur or why your application failed. [Evidence: ATS14, ATS17.]

### Worked example

Someone says “Workable ranks everyone with AI, so your score was too low.” The documentation includes conventional sorting and optional agentic features. Without the employer's enabled mode and actual evaluation, the proposed explanation is unsupported. A useful answer identifies the missing fact and reviews truthful evidence quality without recommending optimization for an imaginary score.

### Identifying the system from an application link

An application address can show which system hosts a posting. Greenhouse documents boards.greenhouse.io followed by the employer's board token, and a hosted job post link that carries a gh_jid job identifier. Current Greenhouse postings are also served at job-boards.greenhouse.io, and a dated observation found the documented address redirecting there. [Evidence: ATS30.] Ashby hosts boards at jobs.ashbyhq.com followed by the organization's slug. Workable's default careers page is apply.workable.com followed by the company's subdomain. Lever-hosted job sites use jobs.lever.co followed by the account's site name, jobs.eu.lever.co in the EU environment, and /apply for the application form. [Evidence: ATS25, ATS26, ATS27, ATS28, ATS29.]

The address identifies hosting, not configuration. Each of these vendors documents ways to show jobs on the employer's own careers page through an embedded board, a widget, an API-driven page or a custom domain, so a company address rules no system out. In one Greenhouse option the candidate browses on the employer's page and applies on boards.greenhouse.io, so the application step can show what the browsing page did not. [Evidence: ATS25, ATS27, ATS28, ATS29.] Once the system is known, read its documented parsing, review and screening behavior in the earlier chapters as possibilities. Which features the employer enabled, which questions and stages it configured and who reviews applications remain unknown. Do not name a system from page design, from an aggregator that reposts the job, or from another employer's setup. Vendors not documented here, such as Workday or iCIMS, are not identified from their addresses by this knowledge.

### Essential, advanced and common mistakes

Essential: identify product, date, configuration, event and evidence limits. Advanced: compare documentation versions and independent employer practice while preserving disagreements. Avoid treating a preview as a released feature, a source archive as current verification, or a narrated demonstration as observed production behavior. Unknown: tenant settings and actual action history. Use workflows D and F.
